Applied AI for Allied Health Professionals

From Clinical Problems to Working Prototypes

Start with the clinical problem — not the technology.

Artificial intelligence is increasingly entering rehabilitation and allied health practice, but clinicians do not need to become software engineers to participate meaningfully in its development.

This developing course introduces allied health professionals to the practical foundations of AI-enabled movement analysis, computer vision and open-source biomechanics.

The emphasis is not on learning programming for its own sake. Instead, participants develop enough technical literacy to define clinical requirements, understand what AI systems are measuring, participate in development, build simple prototypes and critically evaluate whether an output is clinically meaningful.

The course follows a simple question:

I have a clinical problem. How can I turn it into an AI-enabled tool — and how do I know whether I can trust it?

Who is this for?

The course is intended for allied health professionals, educators and researchers interested in participating more meaningfully in the design, evaluation or implementation of AI-enabled clinical tools.

  • Physiotherapists
  • Occupational therapists
  • Rehabilitation professionals
  • Sports and exercise practitioners
  • Allied health educators
  • Clinical researchers
  • Postgraduate students working in rehabilitation or movement science
  • Clinicians collaborating with software, engineering or data-science teams

Prior coding experience: Not required.

Participants are not expected to become software engineers. Programming is introduced only to the level needed to understand, modify, test and critically evaluate simple AI workflows.

What this course is — and is not

What this course is

A clinically oriented introduction to AI-enabled movement analysis and prototyping for allied health professionals.

Participants learn how to move from a meaningful clinical problem to measurable data, a simple technical prototype, an appropriate validation strategy and a responsible implementation plan.

The course develops enough technical literacy for participants to work more meaningfully with AI and technical collaborators without requiring them to become software engineers.

What this course is not

This is not:

  • A software-engineering bootcamp
  • A machine-learning mathematics course
  • Training in autonomous diagnosis
  • Certification in Python
  • Training to produce a regulated medical device
  • Evidence that an educational prototype is ready for patient care

A prototype developed during the course is an educational demonstrator. It is not automatically a validated clinical measurement system.

Five Principles

  1. 01

    Start with the clinical problem

    Technology should respond to a genuine clinical need.

  2. 02

    Understand what the AI is measuring

    A coordinate, landmark or model output is not automatically a clinical finding.

  3. 03

    Translate data into clinical meaning

    Clinical knowledge remains necessary to interpret technical output.

  4. 04

    Never confuse a working prototype with a validated clinical tool

    A system that runs successfully may still be inaccurate, unreliable or clinically inappropriate.

  5. 05

    Keep the clinician in the decision loop

    AI may support assessment and decision-making, but professional judgement and accountability remain with the clinician.

AI in Allied Health: More Than Computer Vision

Computer Vision

Images, video, human movement and pose estimation.

Language Models / NLP

Clinical text, communication, educational support and reasoning support. NLP means natural language processing.

Predictive Models

Risk estimation, prognosis and classification.

Signal Analysis

Wearables, inertial measurement units (IMUs), electromyography (EMG) and physiological data.

Speech / Audio AI

Voice, acoustic signals and communication-related applications.

This course uses computer vision and movement analysis as the primary hands-on pathway because they provide an accessible way to explore the complete development process:

  1. Clinical problem
  2. Data
  3. AI output
  4. Clinical interpretation
  5. Prototype
  6. Validation
  7. Implementation

The principles are intended to transfer to other AI modalities, with their own data requirements and validation needs. Applied AI is broader than MediaPipe alone.

Do You Actually Need to Build It?

Use an Existing Tool

Is there already a validated product that meets the need? Check whether its evidence applies to your intended users and setting.

Adapt / Prototype

Could an existing open-source approach reasonably be adapted for exploration?

Collaborate

Does the problem require expertise from engineers, computer scientists, designers, statisticians or data scientists?

Do Not Build

Does AI fail to add meaningful value, introduce unnecessary risk or solve a problem that could be addressed more simply?

Good clinical AI development sometimes ends with the decision not to build an AI system.

Module 1

From Motion-Sensing Games to Clinical AI

How did we get from gaming technology to AI-supported movement assessment?+

Develop your Canvas: Q1: Clinical Problem · Q2: Intended User · Q3: Clinical Construct · Q4: Observable Data · Q5: Clinical Translation. Revisit these questions using what you learn in this module.

  • RGB and depth sensing
  • Skeleton tracking and exergaming
  • Purpose-built movement measurement
  • Markerless computer vision

Motion-sensing systems such as Microsoft Kinect helped make relatively low-cost full-body skeleton tracking accessible. The following overlapping eras organise the teaching journey; they are not invention dates or a claim that one approach replaced another.

  1. 2010–2015 · Motion-sensing systems

    RGB imaging records colour; depth sensing estimates distance. Skeleton tracking connects estimated body locations, enabling motion-controlled games and exploration of rehabilitation applications.

  2. 2014–2020 · Purpose-built measurement

    The question shifts from “Can the system recognise movement?” towards “Can the system measure movement?” Explore range of motion, gait parameters, postural symmetry and movement performance.

  3. 2020 onward · Markerless computer vision

    Explore ordinary-camera pose estimation with OpenPose and MediaPipe, camera-based exercise monitoring and markerless motion analysis. These approaches have earlier roots: OpenPose’s first public release was in 2017.

The camera has always been watching the person move. What has changed is the intelligence layered on top of the camera.

Timeline background: Microsoft’s Kinect launch · OpenPose release notes.

Your developing project

Think of one assessment or intervention in your own profession where a camera could potentially provide useful information. Select a clinical problem to develop throughout the course.

  2. 02What I currently observe
  3. 03What I would like to quantify

Example: sit-to-stand → trunk compensation → trunk angle and movement symmetry.

Module 2

AI Can See Movement, But Does It Understand It?

What is the difference between tracking a body and understanding movement clinically?+

Develop your Canvas: Q1: Clinical Problem · Q2: Intended User · Q3: Clinical Construct · Q4: Observable Data · Q5: Clinical Translation. Revisit these questions using what you learn in this module.

  • Coordinates, displacement and velocity
  • Trajectories and landmark confidence
  • Clinical interpretation
  • Engineering and anatomical language

What computer vision may output

  • x, y and z coordinates
  • Displacement
  • Velocity
  • Trajectories
  • Landmark confidence

What clinicians may want to know

  • Joint angles
  • Planes of movement
  • Symmetry
  • Compensation
  • Movement quality
  • Functional performance

A keypoint is not a clinical finding.

At which point does the data become clinically useful? Clinical usefulness depends on how the measurement is derived, whether it is valid, and how it is interpreted within the clinical context.

Engineering and anatomy describe movement differently

Engineering language

  • Cartesian coordinates
  • Global coordinate systems
  • x / y / z
  • Rotation matrices
  • Euler angles

Clinical / anatomical language

  • Sagittal plane
  • Frontal/coronal plane
  • Transverse plane
  • Flexion / extension
  • Abduction / adduction
  • Internal / external rotation

The important challenge is not simply detecting points. It is translating technical movement data into clinically meaningful anatomical information.

Practical activity
Describe the assumptions needed to turn a landmark trajectory into one clinically interpretable measure.

Module 5

Human Pose Estimation with MediaPipe

How can an ordinary camera estimate where parts of the body are?+

Develop your Canvas: Q4: Observable Data · Q5: Clinical Translation · Q6: Technology Choice. Revisit these questions using what you learn in this module.

  • Markerless pose estimation
  • Shoulder, elbow and wrist landmarks
  • Hip, knee and ankle landmarks
  • Heel and foot index

MediaPipe Pose can return 33 body landmarks in an image or video frame. These are estimated locations, not automatically validated clinical measurements. The model does not automatically understand “good squat”, “poor balance” or “abnormal gait”.

  1. 01Camera
  2. 02Video frame
  3. 03Pose model
  4. 04Body landmarks
  5. 05Coordinates

Proposed demonstration

Module 6

From Keypoints to Clinical Measures

How do body landmarks become ROM, symmetry or movement measures?+

Develop your Canvas: Q4: Observable Data · Q5: Clinical Translation · Q6: Technology Choice. Revisit these questions using what you learn in this module.

  • Three-point angles and vectors
  • Maximum, minimum and range of motion
  • Duration, repetitions and symmetry
  • Simple compensation measures
  • Missing data, confidence and smoothing

Use hip, knee and ankle locations to form two vectors. The angle between them provides an estimated knee angle; the chosen convention must be explicit.

  1. 01Knee → Hip: first vector
  2. 02Knee → Ankle: second vector
  3. 03Angle between vectors
  4. 04Estimated knee angle

For this teaching example, flexion is 180° minus the included angle, so a straight knee corresponds to 0°. A 2D projection is not automatically a true anatomical angle. Check the camera plane, coordinate scale and landmark definition.

From one frame to a movement

One illustrative frame: knee flexion = 96°. Across frames, plot knee angle over time.

A sequence supports maximum and minimum angles, ROM, duration and repetition counts. Comparing appropriately aligned sides or repetitions may support symmetry and simple compensation measures.

SciPy

pandas

Mini-Lab: From Messy Landmarks to Usable Movement Data

Work with synthetic landmarks only. Retain the raw data and record each decision so that cleaning can be inspected and compared.

  1. Confidence Filter

    Identify estimates with low confidence or visibility. Should an unreliable point be used simply because the model returned a coordinate?

  2. Gap Handling

    Landmarks may disappear because of occlusion, poor camera angle, lighting, movement speed or model uncertainty. Handle short gaps cautiously; some should remain missing. Do not automatically interpolate every missing value.

  3. Outlier Control

    An unrealistic jump in position, velocity or acceleration may be an estimation error rather than true movement. Flag obvious outliers for review rather than deleting them without justification.

  4. Smoothing

    Smoothing can reduce visual jitter. A smoother signal is not automatically a more accurate signal. Excessive filtering may remove genuine movement variability or clinically meaningful instability.

Synthetic example: normalised image x positions (dimensionless, from 0 to 1) at equal time intervals: 0.20, 0.21, missing, 0.89, 0.24, 0.25. Visibility at the second sample is low. Flag that estimate, preserve the gap for review and investigate the jump to 0.89. These values alone do not establish whether interpolation, removal or smoothing is appropriate. The sampling interval is unspecified, so do not infer physical velocity from this sequence.

Proposed output: a short decision log identifying each flagged sample, whether it is retained or left missing, and the reason. If you propose processing, show raw and processed values side by side and state what additional information would justify the method. Keeping the raw values unchanged is a valid outcome when evidence is insufficient.

How much cleaning is too much cleaning? Compare raw and processed data, justify each change and explain which conclusions remain uncertain.

Practical activity
Create a joint-angle series, visualise it and export it to CSV, retaining missing-data and quality information.

Module 7

From Movement to Biomechanics with OpenSim

What can musculoskeletal modelling add beyond visible movement?+

Develop your Canvas: Q4: Observable Data · Q5: Clinical Translation · Q6: Technology Choice. Revisit these questions using what you learn in this module.

  • Musculoskeletal modelling and simulation
  • Scaling and inverse kinematics
  • Inverse dynamics
  • Muscle analysis
  • Assumptions, model fit and data quality

1 — Scaling

Adapt a generic model to the individual.

2 — Inverse Kinematics

Fit movement data to the model to estimate joint motion.

3 — Inverse Dynamics

Combine motion, model properties and external-force information to estimate net joint moments and forces. More detailed loading estimates require additional analysis and assumptions.

4 — Muscle Analysis

Explore estimated muscle-related outputs; these are model-dependent, not direct biological measurements.

Result credibility is bounded by input data quality.

  1. 01Video: image quality
  2. 02Pose: landmark uncertainty
  3. 03Markers: anatomical mapping
  4. 04Motion: model fit
  5. 05Musculoskeletal analysis: forces and assumptions

Error can enter at every stage. Camera landmarks alone do not establish muscle forces. Inverse dynamics needs appropriate external-force data or explicitly justified estimates and suitable model properties. Participants do not need to become OpenSim specialists: the aim is critical interpretation.

Practical activity
Trace a sample modelling workflow and identify the additional inputs and assumptions needed beyond video.

Case 1 — Bilateral Coordination

Consider movement timing, amplitude, trajectory, symmetry and repetition consistency.

Case 2 — Functional Reach

  2. 02Maximum hand position
  3. 03Reach distance

Consider trunk compensation, balance and movement strategy. Distance requires an appropriate scale or calibration.

Is the camera measuring the same thing as the clinical test?

Case 3 — Single-Leg Balance

Consider hold duration, trunk displacement, ankle movement, body sway and movement variability.

Case 4 — Home Exercise Monitoring

  1. 01Prescribe
  2. 02Train
  3. 03Record
  4. 04Track
  5. 05Adjust

Potential outputs include repetition count, ROM, speed, compensation and completion. Pain and fatigue require participant input; adherence and progression require contextual records and clinical review.

The useful system is not simply one that detects movement. It is one that supports a meaningful clinical workflow.

Module 9

A Working AI Tool May Still Be Wrong

How do we know whether we can trust the output?+

Develop your Canvas: Q7: Reference Standard · Q8: Failure Conditions. Revisit these questions using what you learn in this module.

  • Limited frame rate and 2D RGB projections
  • Limb occlusion and imperfect pose landmarks
  • Camera-angle dependency and noise
  • Uncertain anatomical joint centres
  • No direct measurement of muscle force
  • Model assumptions

Level 1 — Technical validity

Does the software run correctly? Check calculations, units, failure handling and expected behaviour; merely running without an error is insufficient.

Level 2 — Measurement validity

Does it measure what it claims to measure? Define the construct and examine reliability and measurement error.

Level 3 — Clinical validity

Does the output agree sufficiently with an accepted clinical or laboratory reference for the intended task and population?

Level 4 — Clinical utility

Does using the measurement help clinical care, education or decision-making in its intended setting?

These four teaching questions guide evaluation; they are not a certification framework.

Working code ≠ valid measurement ≠ useful clinical tool.

A working prototype is not the same as a validated clinical tool.

Challenge the number

Hypothetical AI knee flexion estimate: 86°. What information would you need before trusting it?

  • Camera position
  • Calibration
  • Landmark confidence
  • Reference standard
  • Test-retest reliability
  • Measurement error
  • Movement plane
  • Occlusion
  • Subject characteristics
  • Clothing
  • Task
  • Sampling rate

Practical activity
Draft a validation plan: choose a reference, repeated measurements, acceptable error and populations or conditions in which the prototype should not be used.

Clinician-defined requirements

  • What test is being performed?
  • Which joint or movement matters?
  • What outcome measure is required?
  • What level of error is acceptable?
  • What action should the output support?

Possible future improvements

Explore depth cameras, multiple cameras, higher sampling rates, inertial measurement units (IMUs), electromyography (EMG), clinician-labelled movement datasets, laboratory validation, decision support, home monitoring and privacy-preserving processing. Additional sensors or complexity do not remove the need for validation.

Clinicians should not merely be end-users of AI. They should help define what the system is supposed to measure, how it should behave, and what counts as clinically meaningful performance.

Develop an early demonstrator where feasible, supported by an explicit validation and implementation plan. A well-supported decision not to build is also a legitimate project conclusion; explain the alternative and retain evidence of technical learning from the practical activities.

How Learning Could Be Assessed

PROPOSED ASSESSMENT MODEL

These draft weightings describe a possible assessment model, not a formally approved scheme. They may be refined during pilot development.

  1. Clinical AI Problem Brief 15%

    Participants define the clinical problem, intended user, intended construct or measure, why AI may add value, why AI may not be necessary and the proposed source of data.

  2. Practical Technical Portfolio 25%

    Evidence from selected activities: image processing, pose detection, joint-angle calculation, a movement graph, CSV output, data cleaning and a short interpretation. Assessment focuses on understanding rather than coding sophistication.

  3. Validation and Risk Critique 20%

    Critique an AI-enabled prototype: identify likely measurement errors, limitations, a suitable reference standard, inappropriate claims, privacy and safety issues, and failure conditions.

  4. Capstone Prototype and Defence 40%

    Present the clinical problem → data → AI method → prototype → clinical interpretation → validation → implementation. Include a short oral defence. Where the evidence supports not building, present a reasoned alternative and the evidence that would be needed to reconsider; the practical portfolio still demonstrates technical learning.

“Show me what you built — and explain why you trust it.”

Participants should explain why they selected the approach, what assumptions it makes, what could make the output wrong and what evidence would be needed before clinical use.

For group projects, each participant should explain their own contribution and reasoning during the defence. Identify any AI-assisted code or other external assistance, and explain how the resulting work was checked.

Proposed capstone rubric — five dimensions

Use the following four-level scale for each dimension: 4 — Strong; 3 — Competent; 2 — Developing; 1 — Needs further development. Record a short evidence-based explanation for each rating.

Clinical Relevance
Does the project address a clearly defined and meaningful allied health problem?
Technical Reasoning
Does the participant understand the selected data, method, inputs, outputs and assumptions?
Clinical Interpretation
Is technical output translated cautiously and appropriately into clinical meaning?
Validation
Is there an appropriate plan for reliability, validity, error and comparison against a suitable reference?
Responsible Implementation
Are privacy, safety, bias, workflow, human oversight and limitations considered?

A simple, well-justified prototype should score better than a technically complex but clinically weak system. Coding complexity is not a grading criterion.

Detailed level descriptors and conversion into the proposed capstone weighting remain to be agreed during course development.

Interdisciplinary development workshop
Bring allied health professionals together with engineers, computer scientists, designers and data scientists.

The full ten-module pathway and proposed assessments suit a multi-session course. An intensive workshop would use selected modules and a smaller practical task; its scope and timings will be confirmed before enrolment.

Interdisciplinary development is particularly valuable because clinicians understand the clinical problem while technical collaborators understand how to build the system.

  • Test capstone activities
  • Refine responsible-AI content
  • Pilot with allied health clinicians and/or educators

How the Pilot May Be Evaluated

Proposed evaluation will examine feasibility, participant experience, learning and transfer. No effectiveness claim is made before evaluation.

Feasibility
Completion of practical activities, technical difficulties and time required.
Acceptability
Perceived usefulness, confidence, workload and participant experience.
Learning
Ability to define a clinical AI problem, understand AI output, identify limitations and explain prototype reasoning.
Transfer
Can participants apply the Clinical AI Development Canvas to a different allied health problem?

This evaluation may later support educational research, subject to appropriate approvals and consent.
