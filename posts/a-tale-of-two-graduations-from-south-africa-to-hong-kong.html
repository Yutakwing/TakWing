A Tale of Two Graduations: From South Africa to Hong Kong

Graduation ceremonies have a unique kind of magic, the kind that makes time pause and hearts swell with pride. Sitting in a Hong Kong university hall this week, watching students in crisp gowns and proud smiles, I found myself thinking back to another graduation, years ago, on a different continent.

When I was in South Africa, graduations carried a deep, almost sacred significance for many families. I remember seeing parents, often cleaners, gardeners, or drivers, finish their long shifts and walk straight to the campus, uniforms still on. Some would even take their work badges off and hand them to a co-worker or tuck them away, just to blend in among the guests and watch their sons or daughters take those triumphant steps toward the graduation hall.

It was humbling and powerful. Many of these young graduates were the first in their families, sometimes the first in their entire community, to earn a university degree. I will never forget the tears, the ululations, the way mothers waved proudly from the stands. Every step across that stage represented decades of struggle and perseverance made visible.In Hong Kong, the atmosphere is a little different. Here, education is deeply ingrained; many students are third or even fourth-generation graduates. The celebrations are quieter, the pride perhaps more subdued, but the feeling underneath is surprisingly familiar. Whether it’s a South African mother in her cleaner’s uniform or a Hong Kong father adjusting his son’s mortarboard, the emotion of seeing a child succeed is the same: joy, relief, and a deep sense of fulfillment.

Graduation, I realized, is not just an academic ritual; it’s a moment of human connection that transcends class and culture. Because behind every graduate, no matter where they are in the world, stands someone who dared to hope for a better future, and worked to make it possible.
