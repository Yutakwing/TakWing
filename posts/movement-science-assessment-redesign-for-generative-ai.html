Movement Science Assessment Redesign for Generative AI

How AI-supported preparation, presentation and live oral defence can make student reasoning visible in Movement Science assessment.

Purpose of this note

This note records how I modified the Movement Science assessment in response to generative AI, why I chose not to ban AI, and how the redesign aligns with current institutional and sector approaches to responsible AI use in assessment.

Original assessment problem

The Movement Science task required students to complete a movement-analysis project and present their work.

The task involved areas such as:

  • movement analysis;
  • application of biomechanical principles;
  • biomechanical justification;
  • tissue loading and injury considerations;
  • evidence-based movement modification;
  • scientific communication.

The project/presentation task contributed 30% of the module assessment and included a live Q&A following the presentation.

The main challenge created by generative AI was that a polished final product could no longer be assumed to represent the student's independent understanding.

Students could potentially use AI to:

  • structure the presentation;
  • improve written explanations;
  • generate biomechanical arguments;
  • suggest injury mechanisms;
  • recommend movement modifications;
  • improve language and presentation quality.

Therefore, the final artefact alone was becoming a weaker indicator of what the student actually understood.

Key decision: I did not ban AI

Rather than prohibiting generative AI, I allowed students to use contemporary tools during preparation.

The reasoning was:

  1. AI is increasingly part of the learning and professional environment.
  2. Prohibiting AI does not necessarily develop responsible AI literacy.
  3. The more important assessment question is not simply whether AI was used, but whether the student understands and can defend the final work.
  4. Assessment design should provide evidence of student reasoning rather than relying only on the submitted artefact.

The design therefore shifted from:

"Did the student use AI?"

towards:

"Can the student demonstrate ownership of the reasoning behind the work?"

What I changed

1. Retained the project and presentation

Students still completed the movement-analysis project and presented their work.

This preserved opportunities to assess:

  • preparation;
  • communication;
  • movement analysis;
  • evidence use;
  • biomechanical reasoning;
  • application to practice.

2. Kept AI available during preparation

AI was not treated automatically as academic misconduct.

Students could use AI as a supporting tool during preparation, provided that responsibility for the final work remained with the student.

Potential appropriate uses included:

  • brainstorming;
  • organising ideas;
  • identifying issues to investigate;
  • improving clarity;
  • generating alternative explanations for critical review.

3. Strengthened the live Q&A

The live Q&A became a key mechanism for establishing student ownership of the work.

Questions could probe:

  • Why did you reach this conclusion?
  • Which biomechanical principle supports your explanation?
  • What evidence supports the recommendation?
  • What would change if one variable changed?
  • Would your recommendation still apply in a different clinical or sporting context?
  • What are the limitations of your interpretation?

The Q&A therefore acted as an oral defence rather than simply an informal discussion after the presentation.

4. Shifted assessment from product to reasoning

The assessment was no longer based only on the quality of the final presentation.

Evidence of learning came from several sources:

Prepare → Present → Explain → Defend

This made it more difficult for AI-generated content to substitute completely for student understanding.

5. Human judgement remained central

AI could support the student's preparation, but the student remained responsible for:

  • the accuracy of the content;
  • the justification of decisions;
  • verification of claims;
  • responding to questioning;
  • defending or revising the final interpretation.

The final academic judgement remained with the educator.

Policy basis

1. Saint Francis University — Guidelines on the Responsible Use of AI Tools (August 2026)

Primary institutional basis

The SFU guidelines are organised around the principle:

Protect Data, Exercise Professional Judgment, and Accept Final Responsibility.

The Movement Science redesign aligns with this in several ways:

AI as a supporting tool

AI was permitted as a support for learning and preparation rather than being treated as a replacement for student judgement.

Verification

Students were expected to understand and stand behind the content they presented rather than accepting AI-generated material uncritically.

Human oversight

The live Q&A allowed the lecturer to evaluate understanding directly.

Final responsibility

Students remained responsible for the accuracy and quality of their final work.

Human assessment decision

The final mark and assessment judgement remained with academic staff rather than being delegated to an AI system.

Implication for my assessment design:

AI may support the learning process, but neither the student's reasoning nor the educator's final assessment decision should be delegated to AI.

2. University of Hong Kong — Policy on Use of Generative Artificial Intelligence for Teaching and Learning (2023)

Supporting pedagogical basis

The HKU policy recognises that generative AI creates particular challenges for traditional take-home and text-based assessment.

Relevant principles include:

  • course coordinators may determine the appropriate level of AI use;
  • students should receive clear guidance about permitted AI use;
  • vulnerable assessments should be reconsidered;
  • more secure and authentic forms of assessment should be considered;
  • examples include oral examinations, live demonstrations and presentations;
  • assessment should remain fair and reflect actual student attainment.

The live Q&A in Movement Science therefore aligns with HKU's emphasis on authentic and more secure assessment, particularly the use of live presentation and oral questioning.

Implication for my assessment design:

Rather than relying entirely on an unsupervised final artefact, include an authentic live component that provides additional evidence of student understanding.

3. University of Sydney — Two-Lane Assessment Model

Assessment-design influence

The University of Sydney's two-lane model distinguishes between:

Open assessment

Students may use contemporary technologies, including AI, where appropriate.

Secure assessment

Students demonstrate essential capabilities independently under supervised or controlled conditions.

My Movement Science design can be interpreted as a small-scale version of this idea within one assessment:

Open component

Preparation of the movement-analysis project with appropriate use of contemporary tools.

Secure component

Live presentation and Q&A requiring the student to explain and defend the reasoning personally.

This distinction helped clarify that AI-supported learning and independent demonstration of competence do not need to be treated as mutually exclusive.

The resulting assessment logic

The redesign can be summarised as:

AI-supported preparation → student presentation → live questioning → human judgement

or more explicitly:

Prepare → Present → Explain → Defend

The aim was not to create an "AI-proof" assignment.

The aim was to make the assessment AI-resilient by ensuring that AI use did not remove the need for the student to think, understand, explain and take responsibility for the final work.

Why the Q&A mattered

The live Q&A provided a second source of assessment evidence.

A polished presentation may show what the student can produce.

The Q&A helps reveal:

  • whether the student understands the content;
  • whether they can explain their reasoning;
  • whether they can respond when challenged;
  • whether they can adapt their reasoning;
  • whether they can distinguish strong from weak claims;
  • whether they can take responsibility for what they submitted.

This changed the assessment question from:

"Is this student's work AI-generated?"

to:

"Can this student demonstrate that they understand and own this work?"

Next iteration

The next development would be to add an unseen transfer component after the prepared presentation.

For example:

  • show a short unseen movement video;
  • change one variable in the original scenario;
  • introduce pain, injury or restricted range of motion;
  • ask the student to apply the same biomechanical principle to another movement.

The assessment would then become:

Prepare → Present → Defend → Transfer

This would provide stronger evidence that the student can apply concepts beyond the example they prepared in advance.

Reflection

This assessment redesign changed my thinking about AI in higher education.

The goal is not necessarily to remove AI from assessment.

Instead, the task is to decide:

  • where AI can appropriately support learning;
  • where independent capability still needs to be demonstrated;
  • how students can remain accountable for AI-assisted work;
  • how educators can obtain credible evidence of student understanding.

For Movement Science, the live Q&A became a practical way to maintain that balance.
