Inside a Cell: Teaching Human Physiology with VR

A shared iPad view, a safety monitor and a short race gave small groups a way to explore a virtual cell together.

Share on LinkedIn
VR, Simulation & Educational TechnologyLife as an Educator

A diagram can show students where the nucleus is. In one of my human physiology classes, I wanted to see what would happen if I changed the setting: instead of pointing to the nucleus on a slide, could I ask students to find it together inside a virtual cell?

Cell structures can be difficult to picture when students are learning several unfamiliar names at once. A virtual model gave them a place to look around and a task to do together: find the nucleus, the mitochondria and the other structures we were studying.

One headset, a task for everyone

We split the class into two cohorts of about 30 and worked in groups of roughly four. One student wore the headset. Another watched the person in VR and their surroundings for safety. The remaining group members followed the headset view on a mirrored iPad and guided their classmate.

The iPad mattered because it gave the students outside the headset something useful to do. They could see what their classmate could see, suggest where to look next and help identify a structure. The safety monitor had a separate job: their attention stayed on the person and the space around them.

What the race could tell us

We added a short challenge to see which group could find the most structures in the least time. It gave everyone a clear reason to look closely and talk about what was on the screen. But finding a mitochondrion quickly is a different task from explaining what it does for the cell.

Next time, I’d keep the race short and leave time for three questions: What did you find? How did you recognise it? What does it do? I’d then ask students to locate the same structure on a diagram to see whether they could recognise it in a different format.

I’d keep the small groups, the shared screen and the dedicated safety role. Together, they turned a headset experience into something the group could work on. The session gave me another way to teach an abstract topic, though it didn’t establish whether students learnt more. Next time, I’ll give as much thought to the conversation after the headset comes off as to what happens inside it.
