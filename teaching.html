Approach

Educational approaches

I want students to make their reasoning visible, not only select the right answer. Asking “why?” or “talk me through your reasoning” can expose a missing connection that an expert takes for granted. My reflection on the curse of knowledge considers how educators may hear a complete explanation while learners receive disconnected pieces.

Case work, practical tasks and structured feedback provide opportunities to revisit those connections. The purpose of feedback is to give the learner something to examine and try again, while reflection and oral explanation keep responsibility for understanding with the student. Teaching in practice

How the learning design works

These examples connect the educational problem, the task students undertake, and the reasoning I want to make visible. They describe teaching designs and ongoing work, rather than claims of measured learning gains.

Assessment design

Movement Science: prepare, present, explain and defend

A polished presentation is useful evidence of what a student can produce, but it does not tell the whole story of what they understand. Generative AI makes that distinction especially important. In my Movement Science assessment redesign, preparation with contemporary tools is followed by a presentation and live questioning, so the student must explain and take responsibility for the work.

The live Q&A creates an opportunity to examine intermediate reasoning: why a biomechanical explanation was chosen, how it relates to the observed movement, and whether the student can respond when an assumption is challenged. The emphasis moves from detecting AI-generated material towards judging the student's understanding of the claims they present.

This separates support during learning from the evidence needed in assessment. AI can help students prepare, but it cannot stand in for their explanation during the live component. The design asks students to connect concepts with an example and defend those connections in their own words.

A proposed next iteration is an unseen transfer task: a different movement video or a changed scenario after the prepared presentation. That remains a development direction, rather than an assessment component already implemented or evidence that transfer has improved.

Simulation and reflection

Learning from more than the clinician's role

Simulation offers more than a chance for one student to perform while everyone else waits. My simulation work explores a structured rotation through Learner, Peer Patient, Observer and Peer Debriefer roles. Each role provides a different perspective on the same encounter, with preparation and educator oversight supporting the activity.

The learner rehearses decisions and explanations. The peer patient encounters the interaction from the receiving side. The observer attends to what happens and how decisions are communicated. The peer debriefer helps bring the experience back into discussion. The educational question is what students may learn from each position, rather than assuming that participation alone produces the same learning for everyone.

Focused observation and guided reflection connect the experience to its purpose. Educator oversight remains important for clinical accuracy and psychological safety; peer involvement does not remove that responsibility. The role-rotation study is undergoing analysis and manuscript development. Effects on clinical competence or readiness have not been established.

Purposeful educational technology

Choose the learning task before the tool

AI, digital anatomy and virtual reality have different possibilities, so I begin with what students need to understand or practise. A convincing output or an immersive experience is not, on its own, evidence that a learning objective has been met. The activity needs a clear purpose, an opportunity to respond, and a way to examine the student's understanding.

For AI-supported learning, my practical sequence is: student first, AI second, student explains, verify, then try again independently. The student makes an initial attempt before seeking assistance and remains responsible for checking the response. The independent retry asks whether support has helped them do more of the thinking themselves. This is a learning-design principle, not a promise that every AI tool will produce that result.

The virtual reality acupuncture application is a current development and curriculum-integration project with training, practice and assessment modes. My interest is in connecting spatial and embodied practice with preparation, feedback and debriefing. The project is presented as work in development; an immersive demonstration should not be mistaken for an established clinical training protocol or a proven educational intervention.

Reflection and development

Making teaching decisions open to examination

My public writing records the questions behind these designs: when students need more scaffolding, what an explanation reveals that a correct answer may conceal, and how technology changes the evidence we need from assessment. It provides a place to articulate a rationale and identify the next iteration, alongside the formal responsibilities of curriculum and assessment work.

The faculty-level Teaching Excellence Award in 2026 is a documented milestone in this work. It is recognition of teaching practice, rather than a substitute for evaluating any particular activity. I continue to distinguish a promising design, student engagement and demonstrated learning when describing educational innovation.
